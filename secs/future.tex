\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Across both DAOS deployments, request granularity relative to the DAOS chunk size, not process count or queue depth, is the dominant factor governing throughput, while the process/queue-depth split independently governs tail latency at fixed total concurrency. DAOS scales near-linearly to multi-TiB/s with only tens of client nodes, though per-object overhead leaves many-small-object reads well below the large-object ceiling, and write throughput exceeds read throughput at the process counts typical of streaming ingestion. Access method matters as much as workload shape: native \texttt{dfs} and interception-library access substantially outperform bare \textit{dfuse}, and against NFS, DAOS's RDMA transport delivers an order-of-magnitude throughput advantage at chunk-aligned request sizes that narrows, and can invert, once the workload becomes small-block and IOPS-bound. Together, these results argue for aligning I/O request sizes to the DAOS chunk size, aggregating small events into larger objects before ingestion, and treating concurrency structure, not just its magnitude, as a tuning knob distinct from raw throughput.

In future work, we plan to (1) validate these microbenchmark-derived guidelines on end-to-end scientific streaming pipelines, (2) investigate the intermittent read-throughput variability observed under sustained write-back (Section~\ref{sec:results}), and (3) extend the concurrency and access-method study to erasure-coded, higher-redundancy DAOS containers, which are common in production but were disabled here to isolate raw performance.
