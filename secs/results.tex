\section{Evaluation Results}
\label{sec:results}

Throughout our evaluation, unless otherwise stated, we use the \texttt{dfs} engine for both IOR and \textit{fio}, which bypasses the Linux kernel I/O stack, including the page cache. Each configuration is repeated at least 10 times, and we report the median values. We use the coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean, in percentage) to quantify run-to-run variability. 

\subsection{I/O Concurrency}

\subsubsection{Single-Process I/O Queue Depth Scaling}
\label{sec:iod-scaling}


We run the \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workload on Sys-B. Run-to-run variability remains within 8\% across all configurations, showing consistent write performance. Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod} shows the median throughput and CPU utilization by queue depth. I/O request size has a greater impact on throughput than queue depth. The two small I/O request sizes (4~KiB and 16~KiB) yield consistently low bandwidth of 0.38--0.70~GiB/s and 1.19--2.30~GiB/s, respectively, saturating at $iod{\geq}32$. Large I/O request sizes (1, 2, and 4~MiB) yield bandwidth that rises sharply from $iod{=}4$ to $iod{=}8$ and plateaus at 16--21~GiB/s for $iod{\geq}16$, roughly an order of magnitude above the small-block regime. At $iod{=}64$, the median IOPS is 176~K for $rs{=}4$~KiB and 18~K for $rs{=}1$~MiB, so the 4~KiB configuration sustains nearly 10$\times$ the request rate yet delivers 26$\times$ lower throughput. The CPU utilization clearly indicates that on Sys-B, $rs$=4/16~KiB is CPU-constrained, as it consistently occupies 100\% CPU across all queue depths.

We then fix $iod{=}16$ and sweep the number of \textit{fio} jobs ($nj$). Multiple \textit{fio} jobs/processes are configured to write to one large DAOS object. We repeat each $rs$--$nj$ combination 10 times, and run-to-run variability remains within 12\% across all configurations. As Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_nj} shows, the large I/O request sizes (1/2/4~MiB) are already near-peak at $nj{=}4$, delivering 22.5--22.7~GiB/s (over 95\% of the NIC limit), and remain flat across all $nj$ values, with a slight decline to 21.2--21.5~GiB/s at $nj{\geq}32$. This indicates that for Sys-B, DAOS throughput is client-side NIC-bound: 4--16 processes with $iod{=}16$ saturate the NIC, and additional processes offer no further benefit. In contrast, small I/O request sizes continue to scale with $nj$, saturating at $nj{\geq}16$ with median bandwidth peaking at 6.9~GiB/s and 16.7~GiB/s for 4~KiB and 16~KiB, respectively, 7$\times$ higher than the single-process values in Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}.

According to the DAOS user guide~\cite{daos-user-guide}, the chunk size refers to the largest I/O size that a DAOS client sends to a storage target in a single request. Therefore, for $rs$=2/4~MiB, each I/O operation is broken into several 1~MiB (chunk size on Sys-B) requests, whereas for $rs$=4/16~KiB, more I/O requests are issued while bandwidth remains underutilized. This explains why $rs$ has a greater impact than queue or process concurrency.


\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway:} I/O request size dominates DAOS throughput. Request sizes that are multiples of the DAOS chunk size quickly saturate bandwidth. Scaling both process and queue depth is essential to improve throughput for small I/O request sizes. 
\end{takeaway}


\subsubsection{Tail Latency at Fixed Concurrency}



We evaluate five $nj/iod$ combinations on Sys-B with their product fixed at 256 and $rs$=1~MiB. Every \textit{fio} process writes to an individual object. Each combination is repeated more than 10 times, and we compare the median values. Across all configurations, the mean latency (10.8--11.1~ms) remains consistent, while maximum latency varies from 144 to 165~ms. 

In \textit{fio} reports, latency is further broken down into submission latency ($slat$, reported as minimum, mean, and maximum) and completion latency ($clat$, reported as percentiles P1--P99.99). Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown} shows the mean and maximum $slat$ alongside the P50 and P99.99 $clat$ for all five configurations. The mean $slat$ is consistently low at 0.03--0.06~ms across all configurations. When the per-job queue depth is small (e.g., 4 or 8), the maximum $slat$ spikes to over 1000$\times$ the mean, reaching as high as 45.6--67.9~ms. Completion latency dominates the overall I/O latency. The P50 $clat$ increases slightly as $nj$ grows, while the P99.99 $clat$ decreases. At $nj{=}4$ ($iod{=}64$), P99.99 $clat$ reaches 143.7~ms, approximately 26$\times$ the P50. These results demonstrate that tail latency is strongly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split, even when total I/O concurrency and mean throughput remain comparable. To minimize tail latency without incurring excessive submission overhead, a configuration with more jobs and a shallow-to-moderate per-job queue depth is preferable.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-tail_latency_breakdown.pdf}
    \caption{Tail latency breakdown of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite}.}
    \label{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown}
\end{figure}


\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway:} At fixed total concurrency, tail latency is highly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split. More processes with shallower per-process queues reduce tail latency at the cost of higher submission overhead.
\end{takeaway}


\subsubsection{Multi-Node Scaling}
\label{sec:multinode}


We evaluate \texttt{ior} read-after-write workloads on Sys-A. Despite DAOS POSIX containers on Sys-A defaulting to a redundancy factor (\texttt{rd\_fac}) of 3, we set it to 0 to maximize bandwidth, as our goal is finding the upper limit of a streaming workflow. Following \cite{DAOS-aurora-20-node-test2024scw}, we bind MPI processes to CPU cores using the breadth-skip method.

We examine PPN\,=\,8,\,16,\,32,\,48 and $rs$ of 2/16~MiB, with the \texttt{dfs} chunk size set to 2~MiB. Although the runs are submitted as many separate HPC jobs and thus execute on different sets of nodes ($n\geq64$), run-to-run throughput variability remains within 11\% for all workloads.

Across the full sweep, all eight tested node counts achieve higher throughput at $rs{=}$16~MiB than at $rs{=}$2~MiB, and fourteen of the sixteen $n$--$rs$ combinations reach peak throughput at PPN\,=\,48, with the remaining two peaking at PPN\,=\,32. Fig.~\ref{fig:ior-scaling} reports the 10-iteration median bandwidth along two slices of the sweep: node-count ($n$) scaling at PPN\,=\,48 (left) and PPN scaling at $n{=}128$ (right). DAOS scales near-linearly with the number of nodes before saturation. At a single node, median write and read bandwidths at $rs{=}$16~MiB are 169.2~GiB/s (over 90\% of the compute-node NIC limit) and 108.7~GiB/s, respectively; at $n{=}16$, these scale to 1971.1 and 1824.5~GiB/s, and at $n{=}128$, to 5371.6 and 5507.1~GiB/s. The aggregate NIC bandwidth of the 126 active storage nodes is 5867.3~GiB/s, and the measured 128-client throughput exceeds 90\% of this limit, indicating that the saturation is server-side NIC-bound. The PPN sweep at $n{=}128$ further shows that the larger request size demands less process concurrency: $rs{=}$16~MiB already delivers over 80\% of its PPN\,=\,48 bandwidth at PPN\,=\,8, and is nearly flat beyond PPN\,=\,32, whereas $rs{=}$2~MiB keeps scaling with PPN, with read growing 3.5$\times$ from PPN\,=\,8 to 48. 

Although \texttt{ior} read throughput eventually surpasses write throughput, the crossover requires a large process count: at $rs{=}16$~MiB, read overtakes write at 1024 MPI processes, whereas at $rs{=}2$~MiB it occurs only at the largest configuration (PPN\,=\,48, $n{=}128$, 6144 MPI processes). With hundreds of processes, typical of scientific streaming workloads, write bandwidth is therefore expected to exceed read bandwidth.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/ior-scaling.pdf}
    \caption{\texttt{ior} median read-after-write bandwidth: node-count scaling at PPN\,=\,48 (left) and PPN scaling at $n{=}128$ (right).}
    \label{fig:ior-scaling}
\end{figure}



\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway:} DAOS scales to multi-TiB/s with tens of client nodes on Sys-A, and larger I/O request sizes saturate at lower process concurrency. Write throughput is expected to exceed read throughput for hundreds of processes, and the crossover requires very high concurrency.
\end{takeaway}


\subsection{DAOS POSIX Container Access Approaches}


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/ior_rw_time-nocache.pdf}
    \caption{Per-iteration wall time and transfer time of the IOR workloads under DFS and interception library (IL) access.}
    \label{fig:access-cmp}
\end{figure}

We compare the IOR read-after-write (PPN=48, $rs$=2~MiB) workloads across three DAOS POSIX container access methods on Sys-A, with the Linux page cache disabled.

Plain POSIX access through \textit{dfuse} reads at 3.26~GiB/s and writes at 0.75--0.81~GiB/s, while DFS reads at 74~GiB/s and writes at 107--131~GiB/s, i.e., 23$\times$ or 130$\times$ faster.

The interception library (IL) approach delivers throughput similar to DFS but with much larger variability. Across the iterations of an IL write run, the first iteration is significantly slower than the subsequent ones. We name these iterations write-cold and write-warm accordingly. IOR reports latency per I/O operation. We multiply it by the number of operations to obtain the total data transfer time, and Fig.~\ref{fig:access-cmp} plots the per-iteration wall time decomposed into this transfer time and the remaining metadata time for DFS and IL methods. Metadata time is negligible for reads and write-warm iterations but expands in write-cold iterations: for DFS it adds only 22\% on top of the transfer time, whereas for IL the metadata operations take 2.5$\times$ over the transfer time. Comparing transfer time alone, IL read is 11\% faster than DFS read, while IL write is 2--9\% slower. In the data injection phases of scientific streaming pipelines, where the first/cold write iteration is often the most critical, DFS is therefore preferable to IL.

\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway:} Plain POSIX access should be accelerated by preloading \texttt{libpil4dfs}, which can even outperform DFS access. However, in write-cold scenarios, DFS is preferable to IL, as it avoids the metadata overhead that dominates the first write iteration.
\end{takeaway}


\subsection{Read/Write Patterns}


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-bw-all-pattern-nj16-iod16.pdf}
    \caption{Throughput for different \textit{fio} read/write mixes.}
    \label{fig:fio-all-pattern}
    \vspace{0.5em}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-read-boxplot.pdf}
    \caption{Variation of \textit{fio} read-only throughput.}
    \label{fig:fio-read-boxplot}
\end{figure}



Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-all-pattern} shows the 10-run median throughput of \textit{fio} read/write mix workloads ($nj$=$iod$=16) across five I/O request sizes on Sys-B. Write throughput substantially exceeds read throughput. For $rs \geq$1~MiB, write-only saturates at 22.4--22.9~GiB/s whereas read-only plateaus near 4.6~GiB/s, a roughly 5$\times$ asymmetry. The aggregate throughput for each read/write mix scales monotonically with the write fraction, since write performance dominates the total. 


When $rs\geq$ 2~MiB, sequential and random access converge to within 7\% for all the mixes, indicating that I/O requests at coarse granularity are insensitive to access order. At small I/O request sizes, by contrast, throughput depends heavily on access order. At $rs$=4~KiB, read-related throughput under sequential access is 2--6$\times$ that of random, despite sequential write-only throughput being only 53\% of random. At $rs$=16~KiB, write-only throughput shows a 73\% gap between random and sequential access, while the other mixes stay within 18\%. Overall, small I/O size is consistently order-sensitive, and the direction varies by operation: reads favor sequential access, and writes favor random. 

Read-only workloads also exhibit the largest run-to-run variability among the five mixes. Except for random read at $rs$=4~KiB, all other read-only tasks show 11--20\% variation. Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-read-boxplot} shows the bandwidth distribution for both access patterns, where the boxes indicate the 25th/75th percentiles and the whiskers denote the min and max, highlighting that the slowest runs can drop to half of the median.
Per-run traces reveal two distinct sources of this variability. At $rs$=4~KiB, sequential read shows a classic warm-up ramp, with the first two runs 33\% and 17\% below steady state. At $rs\geq$1~MiB, the low outliers are instead episodic: dips to roughly half the median recur in a few runs, while writes in the same runs remain flat. We infer that background aggregation of the rewritten working set competes with reads for NVMe bandwidth, but leave it for future investigation.


\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway:} Access order matters only at small I/O request sizes, where sequential access favors reads, and random access favors writes. Read-only throughput is also markedly less consistent than that of write-dominated mixes.
\end{takeaway}


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/mpi-fio-read-bar.pdf}
    \caption{The effect of object size on (cold) read throughput.}
    \label{fig:cold-read-large-vs-small}
\end{figure}


Fig.~\ref{fig:cold-read-large-vs-small} shows the read throughput for 1~TiB containers populated with either 16~GiB or 4~MiB objects on Sys-A, using MPI-launched \textit{fio} processes. Throughput is measured as data volume divided by wall time (the latest read-completion timestamp minus the earliest read-start timestamp across all processes), with the I/O request size fixed at 2~MiB, the same as the Sys-A chunk size. Within a single run, throughput is lower on the first iteration and higher on later (warm) ones, and we report only the first-iteration (cold) values.

The baseline 16~GiB/rank large-object read scales near-linearly with node count, from 73~GiB/s at 2 nodes to 457~GiB/s at 16 nodes, showing that distributing objects across more DAOS clients effectively leverages the available bandwidth.
Small-object read throughput is generally lower and depends heavily on the number of objects held per rank. At 4096 objects/rank (16~GiB/rank, matching the 16~GiB-object baseline), throughput reaches only 89~GiB/s at 8 nodes and 91~GiB/s at 16 nodes, a 3--5$\times$ gap versus the large-object baseline at the same node counts. At 2 nodes, 1024 objects/rank (4~GiB/rank) yields 107~GiB/s, 1.47$\times$ the baseline, but at 8 and 16 nodes its throughput falls to 0.7$\times$ the baseline. This shows that the number of objects per rank, not the total data volume per rank, drives the small-object read penalty. This per-object overhead compounds as node count grows, causing small-object throughput to plateau.


\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway:} When reading the same volume of data, large objects scale near-linearly with node count, but many small objects per rank saturate well below that ceiling due to per-object overhead. Therefore, aggregating small detector events into fewer, larger objects is key to scaling event storage.
\end{takeaway}


\subsection{DAOS vs.\ NFS}


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/fio-cmp_dfs-pvsync2_bw-cpu.pdf}
    \caption{Comparison between DAOS and NVMe-based NFS.}
    \label{fig:daos-vs-nfs}
\end{figure}

We copy a 28~GiB file from the NFS-mounted volume to a DAOS POSIX container and use single-process \textit{fio} to measure the sequential-read throughput under two scenarios: (1) DAOS with the \texttt{dfs} engine ($iod$=32); and (2) NFS with the \texttt{pvsync2} engine. For both scenarios, \textit{fio} terminates as soon as it completes a single full read of the file. Fig.~\ref{fig:daos-vs-nfs} reports the throughput and CPU usage across both scenarios, where each bar is the median of 10 runs, and individual runs are overlaid as dots on the throughput bars.

NFS bandwidth is largely insensitive to \textit{fio} I/O request size. It varies only slightly from 0.62 to 0.83~GiB/s across the three tested $rs$. DAOS, by contrast, delivers a median of 10.3--10.6~GiB/s at $rs\geq$1~MiB, over 10$\times$ higher than NFS, but collapses to 0.33~GiB/s at $rs$=4~KiB, roughly half of NFS's throughput. The per-run dots further reveal that DAOS throughput at $rs\geq$1~MiB is bimodal: 3 of the 10 runs form a ``cold'' cluster with a median of 2.9~GiB/s, while the remaining 7 ``warm'' runs reach 10.9 and 10.4~GiB/s at $rs$=1 and 4~MiB, respectively, a 3.5--3.7$\times$ gap. As with throughput, NFS's CPU usage stays within a narrow range (59.7--69.3\%). DAOS's CPU usage spikes to 99.5\% at $rs$=4~KiB, compared to 60.7--62.8\% at larger $rs$, clearly indicating a CPU-bound scenario at small $rs$.

\begin{takeaway}
\textbf{Takeaway:} At or above the 1~MiB I/O request size, DAOS outperforms NFS by an order of magnitude in throughput with lower CPU utilization. At small I/O request sizes, DAOS's IOPS bottleneck saturates the client CPU, and its throughput on Sys-B can even fall below that of NFS.
\end{takeaway}
