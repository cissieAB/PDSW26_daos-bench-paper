We evaluate five $nj/iod$ combinations on Sys-B using a \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workload, keeping their product fixed at 256, with a block size of 1~MiB, a run time of 60 seconds, and the \texttt{dfs} I/O engine. Across all configurations, the mean bandwidth (21.5--23.0~GiB/s) and mean latency (10.8--11.5~ms) remain consistent, while the maximum latency varies widely from 83.1 to 165.0~ms, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-latency}.

In \textit{fio} reports, latency is further broken down into submission latency ($slat$) and completion latency ($clat$), each reported as minimum, mean, maximum, and percentiles (P1--P99.99). Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown} shows the mean and maximum $slat$ alongside the P50 and P99.99 $clat$ for all five configurations. The mean $slat$ is consistently low at 0.03--0.05~ms across all configurations. However, when the per-job queue depth is small (e.g., $iod = 4$ or 8), the maximum $slat$ spikes to over 1000$\times$ the mean, reaching as high as 48.58--59.08~ms. Completion latency dominates the overall I/O latency. The P50 $clat$ increases slightly as $nj$ grows, while the P99.99 $clat$ decreases: at $nj = 4$ ($iod = 64$), P99.99 reaches 149.95~ms, approximately 27$\times$ the P50. These results demonstrate that tail latency is strongly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split, even when total I/O concurrency and mean throughput remain comparable. To minimize tail latency without incurring excessive submission overhead, a configuration with more jobs and a shallow-to-moderate per-job queue depth is preferable over either extreme.
