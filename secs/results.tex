We evaluate five $nj/iod$ combinations on Sys-B using a \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workload, keeping their product fixed at 256, with a block size of 1~MiB, a run time of 60 seconds, and the \texttt{dfs} I/O engine. Across all configurations, the mean bandwidth (21.5--23.0~GiB/s) and mean latency (10.8--11.5~ms) remain consistent, while the maximum latency varies widely from 83.1 to 165.0~ms, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-latency}.

In \textit{fio} reports, latency is further broken down into submission latency ($slat$) and completion latency ($clat$), each reported as minimum, mean, maximum, and percentiles (P1--P99.99). Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown} shows the mean and maximum $slat$ alongside the P50 and P99.99 $clat$ for all five configurations. The mean $slat$ is consistently low at 0.03--0.05~ms across all configurations. However, when the per-job queue depth is small (e.g., 4 or 8), the maximum $slat$ spikes to over 1000$\times$ the mean, reaching as high as 48.58--59.08~ms. Completion latency dominates the overall I/O latency. The P50 $clat$ increases slightly as $nj$ grows, while the P99.99 $clat$ decreases: at $nj=4$ ($iod=64$), P99.99 reaches 149.95~ms, approximately 27$\times$ the P50. These results demonstrate that tail latency is strongly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split, even when total I/O concurrency and mean throughput remain comparable. To minimize tail latency without incurring excessive submission overhead, a configuration with more jobs and a shallow-to-moderate per-job queue depth is preferable.


Notably, the achieved bandwidth across all five configurations exceeds 90\% of the 200~Gbps NIC capacity, indicating that the network link rather than the storage stack is the bottleneck on Sys-B.

\subsection{DAOS POSIX Container Access Approaches}

\subsubsection{\textit{fio}}


Of the two interception libraries, \texttt{libpil4dfs} causes data corruption in \textit{fio} and is therefore excluded. We run a \texttt{randwrite} workload with $nj{=}16$, $iod{=}16$, and $bs{=}1$\,MiB across the \texttt{dfs}, \texttt{psync}, \texttt{pvsync2}, and \texttt{libaio} I/O engines on Sys-A, repeating each configuration 10~times and reporting the median. Via \texttt{dfuse} without any interception library, the synchronous engines \texttt{psync} and \texttt{pvsync2} (each with an effective $iod$ of~1) deliver 39.4\,GiB/s each, while \texttt{libaio} reaches 29.0\,GiB/s. Preloading \texttt{libioil} reduces bandwidth across all three: \texttt{psync} falls to 19.6\,GiB/s, \texttt{pvsync2} to 12.2\,GiB/s, and \texttt{libaio} to 11.1\,GiB/s. The native \texttt{dfs} engine achieves the highest bandwidth at 88.1\,GiB/s, outperforming other configurations by 2.2--8 times.

CPU utilization measurements corroborate the throughput trends. The \texttt{dfuse} engines without \texttt{libioil} and the \texttt{dfs} engine all drive the client CPU above 90\% (99\% and 93\%, respectively). The outlier is \texttt{libioil}/\texttt{libaio}, which consumes only 18\% CPU while yielding just 11.1\,GiB/s, suggesting that the asynchronous I/O queue incurs substantial overhead through the interception layer. Although \texttt{dfs} achieves 88.1\,GiB/s (756\,Gbps), this represents 47\% of Sys-A's aggregated 1{,}600\,Gbps NIC capacity, indicating that \textit{fio} \texttt{dfs} throughput is CPU-bound rather than network-limited.
