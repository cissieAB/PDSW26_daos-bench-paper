
\section{Evaluation Results}

\subsection{I/O Concurrency}

\subsubsection{Single-Process I/O Queue Depth Scaling}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-randwrite_bs-iod_median-bw.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} on Sys-B across I/O queue depths.}
    \label{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}
\end{figure}


We run the \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workload across varying block sizes and queue depths on Sys-B. Each configuration is repeated more than 10 times. Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod} shows the measured bandwidth and median values. Block size has a greater impact on bandwidth than queue depth. The two small block sizes (4~KiB and 16~KiB) yield persistently low bandwidth of 0.38--0.70~GiB/s and 1.19--2.30~GiB/s, respectively, saturating at $iod{\geq}32$. Large block sizes (1~MiB, 2~MiB, and 4~MiB) yield bandwidth that rises sharply from $iod{=}4$ to $iod{=}8$ and plateaus at 16--21~GiB/s for $iod{\geq}16$, more than an order of magnitude above the small-block regime. These saturation patterns indicate that a single-process \textit{fio} job is IOPS-bounded regardless of block size. Run-to-run variability, quantified by the coefficient of variation (standard deviation / mean $\times$ 100\%), remains within 8\% across all configurations, indicating consistent write performance.


\subsubsection{Single-Node Number of Processes Scaling}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-randwrite_bs-nj_median-bw.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} on Sys-B across number of processes.}
    \label{fig:fio-sweep-bs_nj}
\end{figure}

We then fix $iod{=}16$ and sweep the number of \textit{fio} jobs ($nj$), repeating each $bs$--$nj$ combination 10 times. As Fig. \ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod} shows, the large block sizes (1~MiB, 2~MiB, and 4~MiB) are already near-peak at $nj{=}4$, delivering 22.5--22.7~GiB/s (over 90\% of the NIC limit), and remain flat across all $nj$ values, with a slight decline to 21.2--21.5~GiB/s at $nj{\geq}32$. This indicates that for the Sys-B DAOS client node, throughput is NIC-bounded that 4--16 processes with $iod{=}16$ suffice to saturate the NIC for large block sizes, and additional processes offer no further benefit. In
contrast, small block sizes continue to scale with $nj$, saturating at $nj{\geq}16$ with median bandwidth peaking at 6.9~GiB/s and 16.7~GiB/s for 4~KiB and 16~KiB, respectively, more than 8$\times$ higher than the single-process values in Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}. This shows that both $nj$ and $iod$ scaling are essential to improve throughput for small block sizes. Run-to-run variability remains within 12\% across all configurations.



\subsubsection{Tail Latency at Fixed Concurrency}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-tail_latency_breakdown.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Tail latency breakdown of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workloads on Sys-B.}
    \label{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown}
\end{figure}


We evaluate five $nj/iod$ combinations on Sys-B using a \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workload with a block size of 1~MiB and the \texttt{dfs} I/O engine, keeping their product fixed at 256. Across all configurations, the bandwidth (21.5--23.0~GiB/s) and mean latency (10.8--11.5~ms) remain consistent, while the maximum latency varies widely from 83.1 to 165.0~ms. 

In \textit{fio} reports, latency is further broken down into submission latency ($slat$) and completion latency ($clat$), each reported as minimum, mean, maximum, and percentiles (P1--P99.99). Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown} shows the mean and maximum $slat$ alongside the P50 and P99.99 $clat$ for all five configurations. The mean $slat$ is consistently low at 0.03--0.05~ms across all configurations. However, when the per-job queue depth is small (e.g., 4 or 8), the maximum $slat$ spikes to over 1000$\times$ the mean, reaching as high as 48.58--59.08~ms. Completion latency dominates the overall I/O latency. The P50 $clat$ increases slightly as $nj$ grows, while the P99.99 $clat$ decreases: at $nj=4$ ($iod=64$), P99.99 reaches 149.95~ms, approximately 27$\times$ the P50. These results demonstrate that tail latency is strongly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split, even when total I/O concurrency and mean throughput remain comparable. To minimize tail latency without incurring excessive submission overhead, a configuration with more jobs and a shallow-to-moderate per-job queue depth is preferable.


\subsubsection{Multi-Node Scaling}

We run the \textit{ior} workloads with the \texttt{dfs} backend. For a single node, xfer=16~MiB yields peak write of 128.7 GiB/s (PPN=48, about 69\% of the per-node NIC limit) and peak read of 59.6 GiB/s (PPN=8); xfer=2~MiB yields peak write of 101.9 GiB/s (PPN=48) and peak read of 49.7 GiB/s (PPN=32). Scaling to 32 nodes, write bandwidth peaks at 2,168.4 GiB/s (PPN=48, xfer=16~MiB) and read peaks at 2,095.9 GiB/s (PPN=32, xfer=2~MiB), both exceeding 2~TiB/s and demonstrating that Sys-A sustains multi-TiB/s throughput under high client concurrency.

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/ior-scaling-node-ppn.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Peak \texttt{ior} read and write bandwidth on Sys-A.}
    \label{fig:ior-scaling}
\end{figure}

As Fig.~\ref{fig:ior-scaling}(a) shows, the node-scaling behavior of read and write differs. Read at both transfer sizes grows monotonically with the number of client nodes, while write slows from 16 to 32 nodes. xfer=16~MiB yields higher write throughput across all node counts, but for read, xfer=2~MiB outperforms xfer=16~MiB at 4--32 nodes. Fig.~\ref{fig:ior-scaling}(b) shows that the optimal PPN also differs by transfer size and access type. For xfer=2~MiB, both read and write benefit from higher PPN up to PPN=32. For xfer=16~MiB, write bandwidth increases monotonically with PPN, whereas read peaks at PPN=16 and degrades sharply at PPN$\geq$32. Overall, write throughput consistently favors larger transfer sizes and higher PPN, whereas read throughput at scale requires careful co-tuning of transfer size and PPN to avoid degradation.

\subsection{DAOS POSIX Container Access Approaches}

We compare throughput for the same I/O workload across different DAOS POSIX container access approaches on Sys-A. The containers on Sys-A have a default redundancy factor ($rd\_fac$) of 3. We set $rd\_fac = 0$ in all experiments to maximize throughput.

 
\subsubsection{\textit{fio}}

Of the two interception libraries, \texttt{libpil4dfs} causes data corruption in \textit{fio} and is therefore excluded. We run a \texttt{randwrite} workload with $nj{=}16$, $iod{=}16$ and $bs{=}1$\,MiB across the \texttt{dfs}, \texttt{pvsync2}, and \texttt{libaio} I/O engines, repeating each configuration 10~times and reporting the median values. Via \texttt{dfuse} without any interception library, the synchronous engine \texttt{pvsync2} (with an effective $iod$ of~1) delivers 39.4\,GiB/s, while \texttt{libaio} reaches 29.0\,GiB/s. Preloading \texttt{libioil} reduces bandwidth for both engines: \texttt{pvsync2} to 12.2\,GiB/s and \texttt{libaio} to 11.1\,GiB/s. The native \texttt{dfs} engine achieves the highest median bandwidth at 88.1\,GiB/s, outperforming other configurations by 2.2--8 times.

CPU utilization measurements corroborate the throughput trends. The \texttt{dfuse} engines without \texttt{libioil} and the \texttt{dfs} engine all drive the client CPU above 90\% (99\% and 93\%, respectively). The outlier is \texttt{libioil}+\texttt{libaio}, which consumes only 18\% CPU while yielding just 11.1\,GiB/s, suggesting that the asynchronous I/O queue incurs substantial overhead through the interception layer. Although \texttt{dfs} achieves 88.1\,GiB/s, this only represents 47\% of Sys-A's aggregated 1{,}600\,Gbps NIC capacity, and 68\% of the single-node \texttt{ior} peak write bandwidth, indicating that \textit{fio} \texttt{dfs} throughput is CPU-bound rather than network-limited on this platform.
