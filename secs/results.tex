
\section{Evaluation Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{I/O Concurrency}

\subsubsection{Single-Process I/O Queue Depth Scaling}
\label{sec:chunk-size}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-randwrite_bs-iod_median-bw.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} on Sys-B across I/O queue depths.}
    \label{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}
\end{figure}


We run the \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workload across varying I/O request sizes and queue depths on Sys-B. Each configuration is repeated at least 10 times, with the throughput of each run and the median values shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}. I/O request size has a greater impact on bandwidth than queue depth. The two small I/O request sizes (4~KiB and 16~KiB) yield persistently low bandwidth of 0.38--0.70~GiB/s and 1.19--2.30~GiB/s, respectively, saturating at $iod{\geq}32$. Large I/O request sizes (1, 2, and 4~MiB) yield bandwidth that rises sharply from $iod{=}4$ to $iod{=}8$ and plateaus at 16--21~GiB/s for $iod{\geq}16$, more than an order of magnitude above the small-block regime. These saturation patterns indicate that a single-process \textit{fio} job is CPU- or IOPS-bounded. Run-to-run variability, quantified by the coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by mean, in percentage), remains within 8\% across all configurations, showing consistent write performance.



\textbf{Takeaway:} I/O request size dominates single-process DAOS throughput. Sizes that are multiples of the DAOS chunk size quickly saturate bandwidth, while small request sizes remain IOPS-bounded regardless of queue depth.


\subsubsection{Single-Node Process Count Scaling}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-randwrite_bs-nj_median-bw.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} on Sys-B across process counts.}
    \label{fig:fio-sweep-bs_nj}
\end{figure}

We then fix $iod{=}16$ and sweep the number of \textit{fio} jobs ($nj$), repeating each $rs$--$nj$ combination 10 times. Multiple \textit{fio} jobs/processes are configured to write to the same DAOS object. As Fig. \ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_nj} shows, the large I/O request sizes (1 -- 4~MiB) are already near-peak at $nj{=}4$, delivering 22.5--22.7~GiB/s (over 90\% of the NIC limit), and remain flat across all $nj$ values, with a slight decline to 21.2--21.5~GiB/s at $nj{\geq}32$. This indicates that for the Sys-B DAOS client node, throughput is NIC-bounded such that 4--16 processes with $iod{=}16$ suffice to saturate the NIC for large I/O request sizes, and additional processes offer no further benefit. In contrast, small I/O request sizes continue to scale with $nj$, saturating at $nj{\geq}16$ with median bandwidth peaking at 6.9~GiB/s and 16.7~GiB/s for 4~KiB and 16~KiB, respectively, more than 7$\times$ higher than the single-process values in Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}. This shows that both $nj$ and $iod$ scaling are essential to improve throughput for small I/O request sizes. Run-to-run variability remains within 12\% across all configurations.

According to the DAOS user guide~\cite{daos-user-guide}, the 1~MiB chunk size refers to the largest I/O size that a DAOS client sends to a storage target in a single request. Therefore, for $rs$=2/4~MiB, each I/O operation is broken into several 1~MiB requests, whereas for $rs$=4/16~KiB, more I/O requests are issued while bandwidth remains underutilized. This explains why $rs$ has a greater impact than queue or process concurrency.

\textbf{Takeaway:} Larger I/O request size saturates at a smaller number of processes. Sizes way below the DAOS chunk size require both high $nj$ and $iod$ to maximize bandwidth.



\subsubsection{Tail Latency at Fixed Concurrency}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-tail_latency_breakdown.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Tail latency breakdown of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workloads on Sys-B.}
    \label{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown}
\end{figure}


We evaluate five $nj/iod$ combinations on Sys-B with $rs$=1~MiB and the \texttt{dfs} I/O engine, keeping their product fixed at 256. Every \textit{fio} process writes to an individual object. Each combination is repeated more than 10 times, and we compare the median value. Across all configurations, the throughput (22.4--22.9~GiB/s) and mean latency (10.8--11.1~ms) remain consistent, while maximum latency varies from 144 to 165~ms. 

In \textit{fio} reports, latency is further broken down into submission latency ($slat$), reported as minimum, mean, maximum, and completion latency ($clat$), reported as percentiles (P1--P99.99). Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown} shows the mean and maximum $slat$ alongside the P50 and P99.99 $clat$ for all five configurations. The mean $slat$ is consistently low at 0.03--0.06~ms across all configurations. When the per-job queue depth is small (e.g., 4 or 8), the maximum $slat$ spikes to over 1000$\times$ the mean, reaching as high as 45.6--67.9~ms. Completion latency dominates the overall I/O latency. The P50 $clat$ increases slightly as $nj$ grows, while the P99.99 $clat$ decreases: at $nj{=}4$ ($iod{=}64$), P99.99 $clat$ reaches 143.7~ms, approximately 26$\times$ the P50. These results demonstrate that tail latency is strongly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split, even when total I/O concurrency and mean throughput remain comparable. To minimize tail latency without incurring excessive submission overhead, a configuration with more jobs and a shallow-to-moderate per-job queue depth is preferable.

\textbf{Takeaway:} At fixed total concurrency, tail latency (P99.99 $clat$) is highly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split. More jobs with shallower per-job queues reduce tail latency at the cost of higher submission overhead.


\subsubsection{Multi-Node Scaling}
\label{sec:multinode}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/ior-scaling.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{\texttt{ior} peak read-after-write bandwidth on Sys-A.}
    \label{fig:ior-scaling}
\end{figure}

We evaluate \texttt{ior} read-after-write workloads using the \texttt{dfs} access on Sys-A. DAOS POSIX containers on Sys-A default to a redundancy factor (\texttt{rd\_fac}) of 3; we set \texttt{rd\_fac} to 0 across all experiments to maximize throughput. Following \cite{DAOS-aurora-20-node-test2024scw}, MPI processes are bound to CPU cores using the breadth-skip method. 
We examine PPN\,=\,8,\,16,\,32,\,48 and $rs$ of 2~MiB and 16~MiB, with the \texttt{dfs} chunk size set to 2~MiB. Despite being submitted via many HPC jobs and executed on different nodes ($n\geq64$), the run-to-run throughput variability remains within 11\% for all workloads.

Fig.~\ref{fig:ior-scaling} reports the 10-iteration peak bandwidth for each node count ($n$) and PPN. DAOS scales near-linearly before saturation at $\mathrm{PPN} \times n = 4096$: all 8 tested node counts achieve higher throughput at $ts{=}$16~MiB than at $rs{=}$2~MiB, and 14 of the 16 $n$--$rs$ combinations reach peak throughput at PPN\,=\,48, with the remaining 2 peaking at PPN\,=\,32. At a single node, peak write and read bandwidths are 169.8~GiB/s (over 90\% of the NIC limit) and 138.8~GiB/s, respectively; at $n{=}16$, these scale to 1977.0 and 1830.8~GiB/s, and at $n{=}128$, to 5392.4 and 5519.3~GiB/s, demonstrating that DAOS on Sys-A is capable of delivering multi-TiB/s throughput with only tens of nodes. 

Although \texttt{ior} read throughput eventually surpasses write throughput, the crossover requires a large process count: by 10-run median, it occurs at $\mathrm{PPN}{\times}n{=}1024$ for $rs{=}16$~MiB and at 6144 for $rs{=}2$~MiB. Among the hundreds of processes typical of scientific streaming workloads, write bandwidth is therefore expected to exceed read.


\textbf{Takeaway:} DAOS scales near-linearly to multi-TiB/s with tens of client nodes on Sys-A. With hundreds of processes, write throughput exceeds read throughput, and the crossover requires very high concurrency.


\subsection{DAOS POSIX Container Access Approaches}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/iorAndfio_engine-cmp.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth under different DAOS POSIX container access methods.}
    \label{fig:access-cmp}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:access-cmp} compares the 10-run median bandwidth of identical \textit{fio} and \texttt{ior} workloads across three DAOS container access methods on Sys-A. For \textit{fio}, we run \texttt{randwrite} with $iod$/$nj$=16 using the \texttt{pvsync2} and \texttt{dfs} engines. The \texttt{ior} workload is read-after-write with PPN=48 and $rs$=2~MiB. Since \texttt{libpil4dfs} fails the \textit{fio} runs, we fall back to the legacy \texttt{libioil}.


POSIX via \textit{dfuse} delivers substantially lower throughput than DFS across all three workloads. For \texttt{ior} write, the throughput gap is as large as 0.8 vs.\ 133.7~GiB/s; \textit{fio} similarly shows 39.3 vs.\ 88.1~GiB/s. The interception library \texttt{libpil4dfs} significantly accelerates \texttt{ior} POSIX access, enabling IL throughput to outperform DFS by 14--30\%, at the cost of a much larger performance range. \texttt{libioil}, however, degrades \textit{fio} throughput to one-third of the POSIX baseline.


\textbf{Takeaway:} Bare POSIX access via \textit{dfuse} should be avoided when performance is critical. Preloading \texttt{libpil4dfs} can significantly boost throughput beyond the bare POSIX. Native DFS access provides the most balanced performance.


\subsection{Read/Write Patterns}


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-bw-all-pattern-nj16-iod16.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth under different \textit{fio} read/write mixes ($iod$/$nj$=16) on Sys-B.}
    \label{fig:fio-all-pattern}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-read-boxplot.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Boxplot of \textit{fio} read-only ($iod$/$nj$=16) throughput on Sys-B.}
    \label{fig:fio-read-boxplot}
\end{figure}


Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-all-pattern} shows the 10-run median throughput of \textit{fio} read/write mix workloads ($iod$/$nj$=16) across five I/O request sizes on Sys-B. Write throughput substantially outperforms read. For $rs \geq$1~MiB, write-only saturates at 22.4--22.9~GiB/s whereas read-only plateaus near 4.6~GiB/s, a roughly 5$\times$ asymmetry. The aggregate throughput for each read/write mix scales monotonically with the write fraction, since write performance dominates the total. 


When $rs\geq$ 2~MiB, sequential and random access converge to within 7\% for all the mixes, indicating that I/O requests at coarse granularity are insensitive to access order. At small I/O request sizes, by contrast, throughput depends heavily on it. At $rs$=4~KiB, read-related throughput under sequential access is 2--6$\times$ that of random, despite sequential write-only throughput being only 53\% of random. At $rs$=16~KiB, write-only throughput shows a 73\% gap between random and sequential access, while the other mixes stay within 18\%. Overall, small I/O size is consistently order-sensitive, and the direction varies by the operations -- reads favor sequential access, and writes favor random. 


Read-only workloads also exhibit the largest run-to-run variability among the five mixes. Except for random read at $rs$=4~KiB, all other read-only tasks show 11--20\% variation. Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-read-boxplot} demonstrates the bandwidth distribution for both access patterns, where the boxes indicate the 25th/75th percentiles and the whiskers denote the min and max, highlighting that the lowest throughput can be as low as half of the median.


\textbf{Takeaway:} Access order matters only at small I/O request sizes, where sequential access favors reads, and random access favors writes. Read-only throughput is also markedly less consistent than write-dominated mixes.


\subsection{DAOS vs.\ NFS}


\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-cmp_dfs-pvsync2_bw-cpu.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Comparison between DAOS and NVMe-based NFS on Sys-B.}
    \label{fig:daos-vs-nsf}
\end{figure}

We copy a 28~GiB file from the NFS-mounted volume to a DAOS POSIX container and use single-process \textit{fio} to measure the sequential-read throughput under two scenarios: (1) DAOS with the \texttt{dfs} engine ($iod$=32); and (2) NFS with the \texttt{pvsync2} engine. For both scenarios, \textit{fio} terminates as soon as it completes a single full read of the file. Fig.~\ref{fig:daos-vs-nsf} reports the throughput and CPU usage across both scenarios; each value is the median of 10 runs.

NFS bandwidth is largely insensitive to \textit{fio} I/O request size. It varies only slightly from 0.62 to 0.83~GiB/s across the three tested $rs$. DAOS, by contrast, delivers 10.3--10.6~GiB/s at $rs\geq$1~MiB, over 10$\times$ higher than NFS, but collapses to 0.33~GiB/s at $rs$=4~KiB, almost half of the NFS. Similar to throughput, CPU usage stays within a narrow range for NFS (59.7--69.3\%). DAOS's CPU usage spikes to 99.5\% at $rs$=4~KiB, compared to 60.7--62.8\% at larger $rs$, clearly indicating a CPU-bound scenario at small I/O request sizes.

\textbf{Takeaway:} At or above the 1~MiB I/O request size, DAOS outperforms NFS by an order of magnitude in throughput with lower CPU usage. At small I/O request sizes, DAOS's single-target IOPS bottleneck saturates the CPU, and the throughput can be worse than NFS (on Sys-B).
