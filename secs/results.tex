
\section{Evaluation Results}

\subsection{I/O Concurrency}

\subsubsection{Single-Process I/O Queue Depth Scaling}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-randwrite_bs-iod_median-bw.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} on Sys-B across I/O queue depths.}
    \label{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}
\end{figure}


We run the \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workload across varying block sizes and queue depths on Sys-B. Each configuration is repeated at least 10 times. Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod} shows the measured bandwidth and median values. Block size has a greater impact on bandwidth than queue depth. The two small block sizes (4~KiB and 16~KiB) yield persistently low bandwidth of 0.38--0.70~GiB/s and 1.19--2.30~GiB/s, respectively, saturating at $iod{\geq}32$. Large block sizes (1~MiB, 2~MiB, and 4~MiB) yield bandwidth that rises sharply from $iod{=}4$ to $iod{=}8$ and plateaus at 16--21~GiB/s for $iod{\geq}16$, more than an order of magnitude above the small-block regime. These saturation patterns indicate that a single-process \textit{fio} job is IOPS-bounded regardless of block size. Run-to-run variability, quantified by the coefficient of variation (standard deviation / mean $\times$ 100\%), remains within 8\% across all configurations, indicating consistent write performance.

\textbf{Takeaway:} Block size dominates single-process bandwidth: large blocks ($\geq$1~MiB) saturate the bandwidth fast, while small blocks remain IOPS-bounded regardless of queue depth.


\subsubsection{Single-Node Number of Processes Scaling}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-randwrite_bs-nj_median-bw.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} on Sys-B across number of processes.}
    \label{fig:fio-sweep-bs_nj}
\end{figure}

We then fix $iod{=}16$ and sweep the number of \textit{fio} jobs ($nj$), repeating each $bs$--$nj$ combination 10 times. As Fig. \ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_nj} shows, the large block sizes (1~MiB, 2~MiB, and 4~MiB) are already near-peak at $nj{=}4$, delivering 22.5--22.7~GiB/s (over 90\% of the NIC limit), and remain flat across all $nj$ values, with a slight decline to 21.2--21.5~GiB/s at $nj{\geq}32$. This indicates that for the Sys-B DAOS client node, throughput is NIC-bounded such that 4--16 processes with $iod{=}16$ suffice to saturate the NIC for large block sizes, and additional processes offer no further benefit. In
contrast, small block sizes continue to scale with $nj$, saturating at $nj{\geq}16$ with median bandwidth peaking at 6.9~GiB/s and 16.7~GiB/s for 4~KiB and 16~KiB, respectively, more than 8$\times$ higher than the single-process values in Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-sweep-bs_iod}. This shows that both $nj$ and $iod$ scaling are essential to improve throughput for small block sizes. Run-to-run variability remains within 12\% across all configurations.

\textbf{Takeaway:} Large-block throughput saturates at fewer number of processes ($nj$); small-block throughput continues to scale with $nj$, requiring both high $nj$ and $iod$ to maximize bandwidth.



\subsubsection{Tail Latency at Fixed Concurrency}

\begin{figure}[thbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/fio-tail_latency_breakdown.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Tail latency breakdown of \textit{fio} \texttt{randwrite} workloads on Sys-B.}
    \label{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown}
\end{figure}


We evaluate five $nj/iod$ combinations on Sys-B with a block size of 1~MiB and the \texttt{dfs} I/O engine, keeping their product fixed at 256. Each combination is repeated more than 10 times and we compare the median value. Across all configurations, the bandwidth (22.4--22.9~GiB/s) and mean latency (10.8--11.1~ms) remain consistent, while the maximum latency varies widely from 144 to 165~ms. 

In \textit{fio} reports, latency is further broken down into submission latency ($slat$) and completion latency ($clat$), each reported as minimum, mean, maximum, and percentiles (P1--P99.99). Fig.~\ref{fig:fio-tail-latency-breakdown} shows the mean and maximum $slat$ alongside the P50 and P99.99 $clat$ for all five configurations. The mean $slat$ is consistently low at 0.03--0.06~ms across all configurations. However, when the per-job queue depth is small (e.g., 4 or 8), the maximum $slat$ spikes to over 1000$\times$ the mean, reaching as high as 45.6--67.9~ms. Completion latency dominates the overall I/O latency. The P50 $clat$ increases slightly as $nj$ grows, while the P99.99 $clat$ decreases: at $nj{=}4$ ($iod{=}64$), P99.99 $clat$ reaches 143.7~ms, approximately 26$\times$ the P50. These results demonstrate that tail latency is strongly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split, even when total I/O concurrency and mean throughput remain comparable. To minimize tail latency without incurring excessive submission overhead, a configuration with more jobs and a shallow-to-moderate per-job queue depth is preferable.

\textbf{Takeaway:} At fixed total concurrency, tail latency (P99.99 $clat$) is highly sensitive to the $nj/iod$ split. More jobs with shallower per-job queues reduce tail latency at the cost of higher submission overhead.


\subsubsection{Multi-Node Scaling}

DAOS POSIX containers on Sys-A default to a redundancy factor (\texttt{rd\_fac}) of 3; we set \texttt{rd\_fac} to 0 across all experiments to maximize throughput. Following \cite{DAOS-aurora-20-node-test2024scw}, MPI processes are bound to CPU cores using the breadth-skip method. 

We evaluate \texttt{ior} read-after-write workloads using the \texttt{dfs} backend with PPN\,=\,8,\,16,\,32,\,48 and transfer sizes ($ts$) of 2~MiB and 16~MiB, with the \texttt{dfs} chunk size set to 2~MiB. Despite being submitted via many HPC jobs and executed on different nodes ($n\geq64$), the run-to-run throughput variability remains within 11\% for all workloads.

\begin{figure}[thbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/ior-scaling.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{\texttt{ior} peak read-after-write bandwidth on Sys-A.}
    \label{fig:ior-scaling}
\end{figure}


Fig.~\ref{fig:ior-scaling} reports the 10-iteration peak bandwidth for each node count ($n$) and PPN. DAOS scales near-linearly before saturation at $\mathrm{PPN} \times n = 4096$: all 8 tested node counts achieve higher throughput at $ts{=}$16~MiB than at $ts{=}$2~MiB, and 14 of the 16 $n$--$ts$ combinations reach peak throughput at PPN\,=\,48, with the remaining 2 peaking at PPN\,=\,32. At a single node, peak write and read bandwidths are 169.8~GiB/s (over 90\% of the NIC limit) and 138.8~GiB/s, respectively; at $n{=}16$, these scale to 1977.0 and 1830.8~GiB/s, and at $n{=}128$, to 5392.4 and 5519.3~GiB/s, demonstrating that DAOS on Sys-A is capable of delivering multi-TiB/s throughput with only tens of nodes. 

Although \texttt{ior} read throughput eventually surpasses write throughput, the crossover requires a large process count: by 10-run median, it occurs at $\mathrm{PPN}{\times}n{=}1024$ for $ts{=}16$~MiB and at 6144 for $ts{=}2$~MiB. At the hundreds of processes typical of scientific streaming workloads, write bandwidth is therefore expected to exceed read.



\textbf{Takeaway:} DAOS scales near-linearly to multi-TiB/s with tens of nodes. At the process counts typical of streaming workloads, write throughput exceeds read, with the crossover requiring very high concurrency.


\subsection{DAOS POSIX Container Access Approaches}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{figures/iorAndfio_engine-cmp.pdf}

    \vspace{-1em}
    \caption{Bandwidth under different DAOS POSIX container access methods.}
    \label{fig:access-cmp}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:access-cmp} compares the 10-run median bandwidth of identical \textit{fio} and \texttt{ior} workloads across three DAOS container access methods on Sys-A: POSIX mounts the container via \textit{dfuse}, IL augments the mount with an interception library, and DFS uses the native \texttt{dfs} interface. For \textit{fio}, we run \texttt{randwrite} with $iod$/$nj$=16 using the \texttt{pvsync2} and \texttt{dfs} engines. The \texttt{ior} workload is read-after-write with PPN=48 and $ts$=2~MiB. Since \texttt{libpil4dfs} fails the \textit{fio} runs, we fall back to the legacy \texttt{libioil}.


POSIX via \textit{dfuse} delivers substantially lower throughput than DFS across all three workloads. For \texttt{ior} write, the gap is as large as 0.8 vs.\ 133.7~GiB/s; \textit{fio} similarly shows 39.3 vs.\ 88.1~GiB/s. The interception library \texttt{libpil4dfs} significantly accelerates \texttt{ior} POSIX access, enabling IL throughput to outperform DFS by 14--30\%, at the cost of a much larger performance range. \texttt{libioil}, however, degrades \textit{fio} throughput to one-third of the POSIX baseline.


\textbf{Takeaway:} Bare POSIX access via \textit{dfuse} should be avoided when performance is critical. For parallel I/O workloads such as \texttt{ior}, preloading \texttt{libpil4dfs} boosts throughput beyond bare POSIX. Native DFS access provides the most balanced performance across workloads.
